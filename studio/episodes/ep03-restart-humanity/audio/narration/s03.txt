Five years in, Dave and Carla have food and a fence, and a bigger problem. Dave clears his throat. Carla stares at the sea. Neither of them wants to say it out loud. They are two strangers, and the whole future of humanity depends on them getting along. Carla's opinion is clear. But here's the part that surprises most people: the real genetic problem isn't Dave and Carla. Dave and Carla aren't related. Two unrelated people carry different versions of most genes, and that mix protects their children. Their first child could be perfectly healthy. The trouble starts with the next generation. If there are no other people, the grandchildren can only have children with cousins, or with brothers and sisters. And closely related parents can pass the same faulty gene versions to a child from both sides. That's called inbreeding depression: more miscarriages, weaker immune systems, higher rates of disease. And it adds up with every generation. Early humans never faced this alone. They lived in groups of many families, spread across a whole region, and young people moved between bands. Genes kept mixing. That's what kept small groups alive for thousands of years. Dave and Carla have nobody to exchange with. Dave sits down and holds his head. Carla takes the notebook and writes two words: Plan B.
