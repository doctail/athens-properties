# EP02 — What Life Was Really Like in Ancient Egypt (For a Normal Guy)

Status: DRAFT v1 — waiting for owner approval
Target: ~9–10 min · ~1,400 words · narrator Bill (same settings as EP01)
Setting: Giza, around 2560 BC, during the building of the Great Pyramid. Dave is an ordinary farmer.
Format: one day, hour by hour. On screen: CLOCK banner per section (e.g. "5:00 AM"), THREAT callouts, HEALTH meter (replaces the survival meters), final verdict badge.
Rule learned from EP01: cold open under 15 seconds, no rules speech.

Legend: **[V]** visual · **NARRATOR** voice · **DAVE** speech bubble only (no voice)

## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:14)

[V] Dave wakes on a flat mud-brick rooftop at dawn, the Great Pyramid half-built on the horizon, a scorpion walking past his face. Bubble: "…Egypt?"

NARRATOR: It's five in the morning, it's already hot, and Dave just woke up on a roof in ancient Egypt. Before tonight, at least six things here could kill him. Let's see how many he survives.

[V] Title sting: DAVE VS HISTORY.

---

## 5:00 AM — THE ROOF (0:14–1:05)

[V] Village of mud-brick houses, families sleeping on rooftops, the Nile flooding the fields beyond.

NARRATOR: Why a roof? Because Egyptian houses were made of mud brick, and on summer nights, the flat roof was the coolest place to sleep. Dave's house has a few small rooms, almost no windows, and a family of six. Plus the goat.

[V] Dave inside a dark small room, goat staring at him. Bubble: "Hi."

NARRATOR: The year is around 2560 BC. Dave is a farmer, like most Egyptians. But it's flood season. Every summer, the Nile rises and covers the fields for months, so there's no farming to do.

NARRATOR: Which means Dave has been called up for a different job. The biggest construction project on Earth.

[V] Pyramid on the horizon. Dave's face drops.

---

## 6:00 AM — BREAKFAST (1:05–2:00)

[V] Woman pulling round loaves from a clay oven; jars of beer.

NARRATOR: Breakfast is bread and beer. Yes, beer, at six in the morning. Egyptian beer was thick, a bit like a sour porridge, low in alcohol and packed with calories. It was basically liquid food.

[V] Dave sips, makes a face, then shrugs and drinks more.

NARRATOR: The bread is the real danger. Grain was ground on stone, and sand blew in everywhere, so every loaf was full of grit. Over a lifetime, it ground people's teeth down.

[V] Dave bites, a loud crunch, clutches his jaw.

NARRATOR: Archaeologists studying Egyptian mummies keep finding worn-down teeth and painful abscesses. Even pharaohs had terrible teeth. And there was no dentist with anaesthetic. Threat number one: your own breakfast.

---

## 7:00 AM — THE PYRAMID (2:00–3:25)

[V] Huge ramp, teams hauling a stone block on a wooden sledge, a man pouring water in front of the sledge.

NARRATOR: Now, here's what the movies get wrong. The pyramids were not built by slaves.

[V] Stamp "MYTH" over a cartoon whip scene.

NARRATOR: Archaeologists found the town where the builders lived, right next to the pyramids. Bakeries. Breweries. Tonnes of cattle and sheep bones. The workers were fed, housed and organised into teams. Many were farmers like Dave, doing a shift for the king during the flood.

NARRATOR: They were even buried in their own cemetery, right next to the pyramid they built. That's not how you treat slaves.

NARRATOR: The teams even had names. Archaeologists found graffiti left by work gangs inside the pyramids, with names like "Friends of Khufu". Dave's team has a name too. Nobody tells him what it is.

[V] Dave's team high-fives; Dave tries to join, misses every hand.

NARRATOR: And the trick for moving those giant stones? Water. Workers poured it on the sand in front of the sledge. Wet sand is much firmer, and physicists have tested it: it can cut the pulling force roughly in half.

[V] Dave joins a hauling team; the foreman points at the rope.

NARRATOR: But "not slaves" doesn't mean "safe". The average block weighed over two tonnes, and it was moved with ropes, sledges, ramps and muscle. Workers' skeletons show crushed fingers, broken bones and worn-out backs.

[V] Block slips; Dave jumps aside; his sneaker gets flattened under the sledge.

NARRATOR: Threat number two: a two-tonne rock. Dave survives. His sneaker does not.

---

## 12:00 PM — THE HEAT (3:25–4:25)

[V] Blazing sun, workers resting in shade, Dave in his hoodie, steaming.

NARRATOR: Noon. In summer, it could pass forty degrees Celsius. Workers wore a simple linen kilt. Dave is wearing a hoodie.

[V] Dave starts to take it off. A foreman glares. He puts it back on. (Running gag.)

NARRATOR: Dehydration and heatstroke are threat number three. And everyone around Dave is wearing something strange: thick black eyeliner. Men too.

[V] Close-up: worker with dark kohl around the eyes. Dave gets some painted on; looks great.

NARRATOR: It's called kohl. It cut the glare of the sun, and a study found some ancient recipes produced compounds that may have helped fight eye infections. Looking good was also medicine.

---

## 3:00 PM — THE NILE (4:25–5:35)

[V] The river: reeds, fishermen in papyrus boats, Dave kneeling to fill a jar.

NARRATOR: After the shift, Dave heads to the Nile. It's the source of everything: water, fish, transport, life. And it's full of things that want to kill him.

[V] Crocodile eyes rising behind Dave.

NARRATOR: Nile crocodiles, obviously. But the animal Egyptians really feared was the hippo. Huge, fast and very territorial. It attacked boats.

[V] Hippo surfaces under a fishing boat; fishermen flee; Dave backs away slowly.

NARRATOR: And the most dangerous thing in the river can't even be seen. Tiny parasitic worms lived in the water and got into people who waded in. Scientists have found traces of them inside Egyptian mummies.

[V] Microscope-style cartoon of tiny worms. Dave pulls his feet out of the water fast.

NARRATOR: Threat four, five and six. In one river.

---

## 4:00 PM — THE TAX MAN (5:35–6:30)

[V] A scribe sitting cross-legged with a papyrus roll and reed pen, officials with measuring ropes in a field as the flood recedes.

NARRATOR: On the way home, Dave meets the most powerful person in his village. Not a soldier. A scribe.

NARRATOR: Egypt ran on paperwork. Scribes counted every cow, every sack of grain, every worker. And when the flood went down and washed away the field boundaries, officials came out with measuring ropes to mark them again, so everyone could be taxed correctly.

[V] Officials stretching a knotted rope across a muddy field; Dave's tiny plot gets measured.

NARRATOR: There was no money yet. Taxes were paid in grain, cattle, linen, or work. Like the work Dave did today.

[V] Scribe looks at Dave, writes something down, points at a big pile of grain sacks.

NARRATOR: Most Egyptians couldn't read or write. Which means the man with the papyrus decides what you owe. And there's no customer service.

[V] Dave raises a finger to object. The scribe raises an eyebrow. Dave lowers his finger.

---

## 5:00 PM — THE DOCTOR (6:30–7:30)

[V] Dave holding a bleeding hand cut on rope; a doctor with a palette of jars.

NARRATOR: Dave cut his hand on the ropes. Time to see an Egyptian doctor. And honestly? They were some of the best of the ancient world.

NARRATOR: They set broken bones with splints, stitched wounds, and put honey on cuts. That one actually works: honey fights bacteria. We still use medical honey today.

[V] Doctor smears honey on the cut. Dave gives a thumbs up.

NARRATOR: But the same medical texts also recommend remedies made with animal dung. And treatments often came with a spell, to scare off the demon causing the illness.

[V] Doctor reaches for a second jar with flies around it. Dave pulls his hand back.

NARRATOR: So, fifty-fifty. Depends which jar he opens first.

---

## 8:00 PM — NIGHT (6:45–7:45)

[V] Evening: family eating onions, bread, fish; oil lamp; stars over the pyramid.

NARRATOR: Dinner is more bread, more beer, onions, maybe some dried fish. Meat was mostly for festivals, unless you worked on the pyramid crew.

NARRATOR: And life wasn't only work. Egyptians loved music, dancing, festivals, and board games. Their favourite was called senet: a board of thirty squares, played with throwing sticks instead of dice. It was so popular that people were buried with it, to keep playing in the afterlife.

[V] Dave and the family around a senet board; Dave loses; the goat wins.

NARRATOR: Dave loses three games in a row. Against a goat. Technically.

NARRATOR: Then it's back to the roof. Under the stars, next to the goat. And one last visitor.

[V] Scorpion climbing onto Dave's blanket; a little clay amulet of a funny dwarf god next to his head.

NARRATOR: Scorpions and cobras were part of daily life. Egyptians wore amulets and prayed to protective gods to keep them away. Dave has an amulet. Dave also has very fast reflexes.

[V] Dave flicks the scorpion off the roof.

---

## VERDICT (7:45–8:50)

[V] Sunrise over Giza. Dave alive, sunburnt, one sneaker, kohl smudged. Badge: SURVIVED: 1 DAY.

NARRATOR: So, did Dave survive? One day: yes. Barely.

NARRATOR: But living a whole life here is another story. Childhood was the most dangerous part: many children never reached adulthood. Those who did faced worn-out teeth, parasites, injuries and infections, with no antibiotics. Plenty of adults didn't make it far past their thirties or forties.

[V] Badge changes: A WHOLE LIFE? → TOUGH.

NARRATOR: And yet, these were the people who built the only Wonder of the Ancient World that's still standing. With bread, beer, ropes, and teamwork.
